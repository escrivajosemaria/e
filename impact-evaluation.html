Evaluation and impact assessment

Evidence carries weight when it is designed with a decision in mind, from the first question asked to the moment a team changes how it works.

My evaluation work sits inside programme delivery rather than beside it. Most of what I do begins with a practical question from a health team: is coverage where we think it is, why are these households missing services, what is slowing our reporting.

The pages below set out how I move from that question to evidence, which tools I use, what the work has looked like in immunization, community health and MEAL settings, and the checks I run before I trust a number.

From the field

Evidence in practice

Three areas where monitoring and evaluation work shaped what a programme did next.

3,000+Children covered by monitoring and verification activity, with coverage reported at 98 percent

Immunization monitoring

Question
Was reported coverage holding up against what was happening at session sites, and where were children being missed
Method
Daily tally and register review, field monitoring visits during outreach, verification of session totals against reporting forms, coverage summaries compiled for programme review
Tools
DHIS2, Excel, paper registers and tally sheets

High coverage on its own says little. The useful part was checking whether the denominator matched the target population on the ground, and tracing where the remaining children sat.

800+Family surveys, alongside 500+ client feedback responses

Community health data

Question
What did households actually need from community health services, and how did clients experience the services they received
Method
Structured household surveys with digital forms, enumerator briefing on question definitions, client feedback collection, cleaning and analysis of responses for programme review
Tools
KoboToolbox, ODK, Excel

Feedback and survey data read together were more informative than either alone. Where the two disagreed, the gap usually pointed at something worth investigating in the field.

30%Approximate reduction in reporting delays after reporting workflows were reviewed

Programme monitoring and learning

Question
Why were programme reports arriving late, and what was that doing to decisions that depended on them
Method
Mapping of the reporting flow from field team to programme level, review of indicator definitions and templates, follow up on the steps where data was stalling, regular monitoring reports built into the MEAL routine
Tools
Excel, Power BI, DHIS2

The delay was structural rather than a matter of effort. Simplifying what had to be submitted, and making clear who submitted it, moved reports forward by roughly a third of the usual lag.

Evaluation logic

Activity, output, outcome, impact

Programme reporting often stops at the first two. Evaluation exists to ask about the last two, carefully.

Activity
What the programme does
The work itself, planned and carried out by the team.
Outreach immunization sessions are held in selected villages, with community mobilisation before each session.
Output
What was delivered
Countable results of the activity, usually available from routine records.
Sessions held, children vaccinated, doses administered, households visited.
Outcome
What changed
A shift in coverage, behaviour or service use that the outputs were meant to produce.
A higher share of children in the catchment completing the schedule on time, and fewer defaulters between doses.
Impact
The longer term difference
Change in health status over time, shaped by many factors beyond one programme.
Reduced incidence of vaccine preventable illness in the population served.

The further right the chain goes, the harder attribution becomes. Outputs belong to the programme. Outcomes are influenced by it. Impact sits inside a wider system of services, seasons, migration and household circumstances, so I report contribution and describe the other plausible explanations rather than claiming a programme caused a population level change.

Data quality

Before I trust the number

The checks that run before a figure enters a report. Most weak findings fail at one of these.

  • Is the indicator clearly defined?
    Everyone collecting the data should be counting the same thing in the same way.
  • Is the denominator right?
    Target population estimates age quickly, and a wrong denominator can manufacture impressive coverage.
  • Is the source appropriate for the question?
    Routine data answers different questions from a survey, and mixing them without saying so is misleading.
  • What is missing, and is the missingness patterned?
    Gaps clustered in one site or one week usually mean something other than random error.
  • Has the data been verified against source records?
    Registers, tally sheets and submitted totals should agree before analysis begins.
  • Is the time period consistent?
    Comparisons break quietly when reporting periods or cut off dates shift between sites.
  • Are there anomalies worth explaining?
    Sudden jumps, duplicates and impossible values get traced before they are interpreted.
  • Does the result make sense to people in the field?
    If the figure surprises the team that produced it, the reason needs finding first.
  • Can the evidence actually support the conclusion?
    Where it cannot, the honest answer is a narrower finding and a note on what would be needed to go further.

This list is short by design. It is the review I run on my own work before anyone else sees it, and the same questions I ask when reviewing data produced by a wider team.

Philosophy

Evidence designed for use

Evaluation works best as a learning and decision support function rather than a compliance exercise. The strongest evaluations begin with clarity on why the evidence is needed, who will use it, and what will be done differently once it arrives.

Methodological care, ethical standards and contextual relevance matter equally. Designs stay feasible, transparent and honest about their limits, because a method that cannot be executed properly in the field produces findings nobody can defend.

Framework

Four stages of design

Evaluations are structured so that programme objectives connect to results that can actually be measured.

01
Framework design
Questions, criteria and indicators are agreed at the outset so they align with programme goals, stakeholder priorities and reporting requirements. A simple theory of change makes the assumptions visible.
02
Data collection
Quantitative and qualitative methods are combined to capture both performance trends and the context around them, using surveys, interviews, group discussions and document review.
03
Analysis
The emphasis falls on interpretation rather than description. Patterns, trends, disparities and unintended effects are examined to explain what is driving performance.
04
Reporting and use
Findings are synthesised into clear outputs that support decisions, with the emphasis on implications, recommendations and practical options for adaptation.
Areas of practice

Five core domains

Where evaluation, monitoring and evidence translation meet programme decision making.

1
Programme evaluation
Assessing the relevance, effectiveness and efficiency of health programmes against their objectives and the populations they serve, with attention to what the evidence means for practice.
2
Impact assessment
Examining short and longer term outcomes to understand how interventions contribute to changes in health behaviour, service use and system performance at community and facility level.
3
Monitoring and performance analysis
Using routine data and indicators to track progress, flag risks and guide timely corrective action, so programmes can respond while there is still time to respond.
4
Evidence based recommendations
Translating findings into practical actions that inform planning, resource allocation and implementation, closing the distance between what the data shows and what decision makers need.
5
Capacity building in evaluation
Strengthening monitoring and evaluation skills among teams and stakeholders so that data quality, learning and accountability become part of routine programme culture.
