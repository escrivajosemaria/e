Overview

Community outreach connects health systems with the people they are meant to serve. Many preventable health problems persist because of barriers to access, awareness, trust, and social norms, not because services are missing. Outreach brings information and services closer to people, supports health-seeking behaviour, and keeps programmes grounded in local realities. The World Health Organization treats community engagement as essential to improving health outcomes, service uptake, and the resilience of health systems.

Youth health is a particular focus. Adolescents represent over 1.2 billion people globally, moving through rapid physical, emotional, and social change that leaves them exposed to preventable health risks. According to the World Health Organization, many of the leading causes of illness and death among adolescents can be prevented with timely information, supportive environments, and services they can actually use.

01Mentorship stories

Where it all began: street outreach in Nairobi

My first real exposure to mentorship did not happen in a classroom or a conference room. It happened on the streets of Nairobi, working alongside BWAB Kenya. That was my introduction to mentoring children surviving in environments shaped by poverty, displacement, substance use, and social exclusion.

Trust is not given because you have a badge or a good heart. It is earned slowly, sometimes through laughter, sometimes through silence.

Sessions covered drug use sensitisation, disease prevention, hygiene, and personal safety. Mentorship went past the information, though. It meant listening to stories that do not fit into reports, understanding survival behaviours, and realising that resilience often looks nothing like success as textbooks define it.

02Mentorship stories

Primary school mentorship: foundations matter

Mentoring primary school children taught me that prevention really does begin early. At this level, mentorship is less about instruction and more about shaping curiosity, confidence, and self-worth. Sessions often mixed health education with play, storytelling, and simple reflection exercises.

The biggest challenge was keeping attention longer than ten minutes. That part humbles you fast.

Health topics covered hygiene, nutrition, safety, and early awareness of substance use. More usefully, the sessions helped children see themselves as capable of making good decisions, even where their environments make that hard.

According to the World Health Organization, early life interventions strongly influence long-term health behaviours, educational outcomes, and psychosocial development.

Primary-level mentorship programs typically engaged 40 to 60 learners per cycle, with repeated sessions over school terms to reinforce learning and trust.
03Mentorship stories

High school mentorship: identity, pressure, and choice

High school mentorship is where conversations get real, fast. Adolescents are working through identity, expectations, peer pressure, and uncertainty about the future, usually all at once. Sessions focused on mental health awareness, substance use prevention, sexual and reproductive health, and decision-making.

Lecturing does not work. Dialogue does. The best sessions started with one honest question and ended with students teaching each other.

The WHO identifies mental health conditions as one of the leading causes of illness among adolescents globally, which is why school and community based support matters so much.

Typical high school programs I supported reached 80 to 120 students per engagement cycle, combining group mentorship with smaller discussion spaces for sensitive topics.
04Mentorship stories

Campus mentorship: from guidance to partnership

Mentoring university and college students feels less like instruction and more like collaboration. The work shifts toward navigating systems, managing pressure, career uncertainty, and holding onto mental well-being while moving into adulthood.

Discussions often covered leadership, community service, health advocacy, and balancing ambition with rest. My approach stays adaptive, reflective, and grounded in listening.

Mentorship at this level runs both ways. I learn as much as I give.

Research consistently shows that mentorship in higher education improves retention, confidence, and long-term professional outcomes. National Institutes of Health ↗

Campus mentorship engagements typically involved 30 to 50 students per cohort, often supported by peer mentors including James Mwangi, Aisha Noor, Kevin Otieno, and Ruth Wambui.
Intelligence, potential, and kindness are not distributed according to opportunity. They exist everywhere. Access does not.
On what mentorship gave back

Access is not automatic. It is built.

Young people do not skip services because they are uninterested. They skip them because the systems are complicated, intimidating, and absent from the places where young people actually spend their time. Digital engagement closes some of that distance: it moves faster than misinformation and reaches people where they already are. During a rapid response campaign with UNDP after the Myanmar earthquake, I worked on online risk communication that put clarity, accessibility, and tone first.
