§01 Foundation

Health policy extends beyond the drafting of documents. It encompasses decision-making, the allocation of power, the creation of incentives, and the navigation of trade-offs that shape how health systems function. Effective governance ensures that these policies are translated into action through accountability, coordination, stewardship, and compliance mechanisms.

Even the most well-designed policies can fail without strong governance structures. Conversely, governance mechanisms cannot compensate for policies that are unclear, misaligned, or lacking in strategic direction. Understanding the interplay between policy content and governance processes is critical to strengthening health systems and ensuring sustainable outcomes.

§02 Practice

Four lenses on applied governance

How policy and governance are approached in practice, from analysis through to advisory.

Policies are carefully examined before any action. This involves assessing their objectives, the evidence underpinning them, and potential impacts on program operations. Analysis ensures interventions are grounded in data and aligned with broader system priorities.

Governance frameworks are implemented with care to maintain compliance without restricting necessary flexibility. Understanding legal, operational, and ethical boundaries ensures that programs adhere to standards while adapting to local realities.

Decision-making under real-world constraints requires balancing evidence, operational realities, and stakeholder interests. Adaptive strategies ensure programs remain effective and responsive to changing conditions and emerging challenges.

Programs are supported with strategic guidance without overstepping mandates. This involves advising on resource allocation, operational priorities, and governance strategies to strengthen overall system performance.

§03 Governance failures

Governance problems this work addresses

Select a problem to see the governance response. The line between failure and solution is where applied governance work lives.

01
Policies not implemented

Policies often exist on paper but fail in practice due to unclear responsibilities or weak enforcement mechanisms at the operational level.

Response

Governance support ensures policies are operationalized effectively and linked to measurable outcomes, through clear accountability chains and implementation monitoring.

02
Weak accountability mechanisms

Without transparent oversight, programs deviate from intended outcomes. Reporting systems fail. Supervision is inconsistent. Feedback never reaches decision-makers.

Response

Strengthening reporting systems, supervision structures, and feedback loops reinforces accountability across all levels of the health system.

03
Fragmented decision-making

Multiple actors with unclear authority slow implementation. Decisions stall. Resources wait. Communities wait longer.

Response

Governance support aligns roles, clarifies mandates, and facilitates coordinated decision-making that keeps programs moving.

04
Programs misaligned with national priorities

Programs that bypass strategic priorities risk wasting resources and duplicating effort, undermining the very systems they aim to support.

Response

Governance guidance ensures alignment with national health strategies and long-term system goals, from program design through evaluation.

05
Data not used for governance

Data is collected but rarely makes it into decisions. Reports go unread. Insights go unacted upon. The learning loop stays broken.

Response

Governance support integrates evidence into strategic planning, supervision cycles, and policy adjustments, closing the loop between data and decision.

§05 Policy in practice

A working position on where policy actually becomes visible, written from the implementation side of the health system rather than the drafting side.

Policy is where evidence meets implementation

Much of my policy work has happened downstream from the policy document itself, where evidence has to become a programme decision, a monitoring framework, a community intervention, or a message that people can actually use.

Coordinating public health programmes teaches you quickly that a policy is only as good as the arrangements that carry it. A vaccination schedule is a technical instruction until someone decides who reports what, on which day, to which office, and what happens when the numbers do not arrive. That gap between intent and delivery is where most of my practical governance work sits, and it is the reason I treat policy as an operational question before treating it as a written one.

Evidence is the part that holds it together. Coverage surveys, routine monitoring through platforms such as DHIS2 and KoboToolbox, supervision notes, and community feedback all describe the same system from different angles. Reading them together usually tells you something a single report cannot: whether a shortfall is a supply problem, a coordination problem, or a trust problem, and therefore whether the right response is a logistics fix, a governance fix, or a conversation.

Working in monitoring, evaluation, accountability and learning, including immunization related work in WHO supported settings, has made me careful about what data can and cannot carry. Indicators are decisions in compressed form. What gets counted shapes what gets funded, supervised, and eventually believed, so designing a reporting line is itself a small act of policy, and it deserves the same scrutiny as the strategy it serves.

The other half of the work is communication. Evidence that stays inside a report changes nothing. Turning findings into briefs, campaign material, visual summaries, and plain explanations for community health teams and partners is not a communications afterthought; it is how a policy position survives contact with the people expected to act on it.

Evidence

Research, coverage surveys, and routine programme data used to describe what is actually happening, not what was planned.

Implementation

Translating decisions into schedules, responsibilities, supervision routines, and community-level activity that can be delivered.

Monitoring and learning

Reporting frameworks and review cycles designed so that findings return to the people with the authority to change something.

Coordination

Aligning facilities, outreach teams, local leadership, and partners around a shared plan rather than parallel ones.

Communication

Reports, briefs, and campaign material that make a decision legible to the people expected to carry it out.

§06 Frameworks and practice

Notes from volunteer and development programme environments, where global goals have to be restated as something a county, a facility, or a youth group can act on.

From global frameworks to local practice

Global development frameworks are written to be universal, which is exactly what makes them difficult to use. Someone has to decide what a goal means in a particular county, with a particular budget, in a particular season.

Working in United Nations Volunteers and UNDP programme environments gave me a fairly unsentimental view of that translation. The Sustainable Development Goals arrive as language. They leave as a training session, a data collection round, a partner meeting, a piece of advocacy material, or a youth-led activity in a community that has never read the framework and does not need to.

Volunteerism sits close to the centre of this. It is often described as a contribution of labour, but in practice it is a governance mechanism: it brings local knowledge into programmes that would otherwise be designed at a distance, and it gives young people a legitimate route into planning, monitoring, and advocacy rather than only into delivery. Youth development work has consistently been the point where the framework stops being abstract.

My contribution in these settings has mostly been on the evidence and communication side, coordinating activities, supporting research and analysis, preparing reports and knowledge products, and producing material that makes programme work visible to partners and to the communities involved. Visibility is not vanity here. Documented work is what makes a partnership possible, what makes a lesson transferable, and what keeps a commitment reviewable after the launch event.

Development and humanitarian contexts also impose their own discipline. Plans change quickly, and the useful skill is less the ability to produce a perfect framework than the ability to keep coordination, reporting, and stakeholder relationships intact while the situation moves. That is the part of governance that rarely appears in policy documents and almost always decides whether they hold.
