Overview

From field reality to informed decision

Data collection and reporting are foundational to effective health systems and programs, providing the evidence needed to understand what is working, what is not, and where adjustments are required. Reliable data enables health actors to move beyond assumptions toward informed decision-making that improves service delivery, accountability, and population health outcomes.

Reporting is approached not as a compliance exercise, but as a learning tool. Clear, well-structured reports transform raw data into actionable insights that program teams, managers, and partners can understand and use. By analyzing trends, identifying gaps, and highlighting performance patterns, reporting supports continuous improvement and adaptive management.

Scope of data collection

Data collection activities are structured to support Monitoring, Evaluation, Accountability, and Learning processes across the project lifecycle, including routine monitoring data from service delivery points, periodic surveys and assessments, and qualitative data gathered through interviews, focus group discussions, and participatory approaches.

Monitoring frameworks and indicators

Frameworks are developed using clear results chains and logical frameworks that link inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, and impact. Indicators are selected to be SMART and aligned with donor requirements, national health information systems, and global standards such as those outlined by the World Health Organization.

Data quality assurance

Data quality assurance measures include standardised tools, routine data verification, consistency checks, supervision support, and periodic data quality assessments, aligning with global MEAL guidance from organisations such as ALNAP.

02
Approach

The data cycle

Data and reporting are treated as integrated parts of program management. Each stage exists to keep evidence relevant, reliable, and genuinely used, for decisions, for learning, and for accountability.

01

Assess

Understanding data needs, decision-making context, and existing information systems, examining program objectives, management questions, and reporting requirements to determine what evidence is genuinely required before any collection begins.

02

Design

Creating indicators, tools, and workflows that are analytically rigorous but feasible under real-world conditions. Systems perform best when simple, standardized, and embedded within routine service delivery rather than run as parallel structures.

03

Implement

Collecting, cleaning, and validating data as a continuous quality process, not a one-time exercise. Most data quality problems trace back to ambiguous definitions, time constraints, and misaligned incentives: routine checks and ongoing supervision catch errors early.

04

Analyse

Generating disaggregated insights that explain results and surface equity gaps, prioritizing interpretation over description. Visualization is used deliberately, to reduce the reader's cognitive load rather than to impress.

05

Report

Delivering findings with embedded feedback loops, so reporting functions as a decision-support mechanism rather than a static document. Reports emphasize implications, risks, and opportunities, and feed directly back into planning and resource allocation.

Applied projects: Trans-Nzoia County

Evidence in the field

Beyond frameworks and theory, this work is grounded in real implementation under St. Martha Hospital-supported initiatives. Three projects below show how data shaped decisions in the field.

Malaria prevention

Mosquito net utilisation campaign

A rapid household survey and community dialogue revealed heat discomfort and damaged nets as the key barriers to use. The findings informed targeted health education delivered through community health volunteers. Follow-up monitoring showed a measurable increase in net usage among households with children under five.

Non‑communicable disease

NCD awareness & community screening

A knowledge survey was administered at community forums and market-day events. Findings showed general NCD awareness was moderate, but understanding of why screening mattered remained low, a gap that guided simplified health talks and mobile screening days.

Youth health

Youth preventive health awareness

Anonymous surveys revealed that peer-led, school-linked activities significantly outperformed facility-based messaging. The findings shifted outreach delivery toward youth participation and localized messaging, away from generic health-promotion material.

Presenting the evidence

Four principles guide how findings from these projects are turned into dashboards and reports that management and partner audiences can actually use.

Clarity

Focus on the information that matters most for the decision at hand.

Hierarchy

Guide attention to critical insights using layout, contrast, and emphasis.

Simplicity

Simplify complex metrics without losing analytical rigour.

Context

Pair quantitative data with narrative interpretation.
