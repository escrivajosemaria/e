About

Escriva works in public health, mostly across Kenya, moving between hospitals, community programmes and the humanitarian organisations that step in when systems are stretched thin. His days sit between spreadsheets and clinics, between what a programme reports and the people those reports are meant to represent.

01Practice

At the intersection of systems, data and community

Escriva has worked across hospital systems, NGOs, UN-affiliated programmes and grassroots organisations in Kenya: on community health strengthening, emergency response, vaccination campaigns, and the monitoring and evaluation systems that keep a programme honest about what it is actually achieving.

That work has run through WHO polio eradication campaigns, UNDP communications and outreach, and Kenya Red Cross community health promotion, usually sitting between hospitals, civil society groups and local government as they try to align what they're doing with the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly around maternal and child health, mental health and the resilience of the systems holding it all together.

Writing has become part of that practice too. He has contributed to academic and professional publications on community mental health, development policy and applied public health, work that tries to turn field research and mixed-methods analysis into something a programme team can actually use.

02Method

Bridging data, policy and lived experiences

Data systems don't mean much sitting on their own. Escriva's work treats them as a way of listening: to what communities are reporting, to what's changing on the ground, and to the gap between what a policy assumes and what people are actually living through.

That means holding two things at once, quantitative evidence next to what a conversation in a clinic waiting room tells you, institutional requirements next to community realities, global frameworks next to what's actually true of a particular estate or village. Real-time data, community feedback and participatory decision-making, read together, are what let a programme move from technically correct to something a community will trust and keep going.

Underneath that process is a fairly simple idea: public health only works if dignity and equity are starting points rather than outcomes, and if the people running it stay willing to keep learning rather than defend a plan that stopped fitting the situation.

03Perspective

Learning to build from the ground up

For a while, Escriva's work was mostly about showing up: helping run a vaccination day, sitting in on a community meeting, collecting the data a programme needed. Over time that shifted. Through UN Volunteers and UNDP-linked work, WHO immunisation and MEAL support, and later through building LeafSpire from nothing as a youth mentorship initiative, the question changed from how do I help deliver this to how would I design this.

Founding LeafSpire made that shift concrete. Deciding what a mentorship programme should actually do, how to talk about it publicly, how to track whether it's working and adjust when it isn't, is a different kind of work from executing someone else's plan. It also made the earlier programme and research experience useful in a new way: the monitoring frameworks he'd applied to other people's projects became tools he had to build and defend for his own.

If there's a lesson in that arc, it's that change in this field rarely comes from a single intervention done well. It comes from paying attention long enough, across enough programmes and enough conversations with the people those programmes are meant to serve, to notice what actually moves and what just looks like it does on a report.
